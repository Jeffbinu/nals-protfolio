When people hear "OCR," they usually picture a clean PDF and `pytesseract.image_to_string()`. The reality of large-scale government document extraction looks nothing like that.

Our task: extract structured fields from **60+ million scanned voter roll pages**, printed in Tamil, across decades of inconsistent scanners, print qualities, and layouts. Some pages were crisp. Many were skewed, faded, or photographed at an angle from a photocopier lid. A single OCR engine — no matter how good — was never going to hit acceptable accuracy across that entire distribution.

## Why one engine wasn't enough

Early on, we benchmarked three OCR engines against a labeled sample:

- **Tesseract** — fast, reliable on clean, high-contrast scans, but brittle on Tamil ligatures under noise.
- **Surya OCR** — strong on complex, multi-column layouts, but slower per page.
- **PaddleOCR** — surprisingly resilient on low-contrast and slightly rotated scans.

No engine dominated. Each had a "zone of competence" tied to specific document conditions. So instead of picking a winner, we built a system that picks the winner *per document*.

## The preprocessing layer matters as much as the model

Before any engine sees a page, it goes through an OpenCV preprocessing stage:

```python
def preprocess(image):
    image = deskew(image)
    image = denoise(image)
    image = enhance_contrast(image)
    return image
```

De-skewing alone recovered a meaningful chunk of accuracy on photocopied pages, where even a 2-3 degree tilt was enough to break character segmentation. Contrast enhancement helped the most on older scans where toner had faded unevenly across the page.

## An adaptive OCR selection strategy

Rather than running all three engines on every page (expensive, and unnecessary), we classify each document by a handful of cheap signals — estimated noise level, contrast variance, layout complexity — and route it to the engine most likely to perform well on that profile. Lower-confidence outputs get escalated to a second engine as a fallback pass.

This is the part that actually moved the needle: it's not about having the best individual model, it's about knowing *when* to trust which model.

## Making it fast: Docker microservices

Accuracy was only half the problem. At 60 million records, even a small per-page latency compounds into days of processing time. We containerized each OCR engine as an independent microservice and ran them behind a job queue, allowing horizontal scaling and parallelized, distributed processing across the corpus.

The result: extraction of 100,000 records dropped from what would have been well over half a day of naive processing to **90 minutes**.

## What I'd tell someone starting a similar project

1. Benchmark on your actual data distribution, not a clean sample — the edge cases *are* the dataset.
2. Preprocessing is not optional. It's often cheaper to fix the image than to fix the model.
3. Design for graceful degradation. A pipeline that fails loudly on 5% of documents is more useful than one that silently guesses.
4. Optimize for parallelism early — a correct pipeline that can't scale is still an unsolved problem at production volume.
